Section 1: Refrigerant Cylinders & Safe Handling (11.1)

  1. Refillable DOT tanks used to store used refrigerant taken out of a unit are called recovery cylinders and are painted gray on the lower body with a yellow top/shoulder.
  2. Standard recovery tanks typically feature a dual-port Y-valve assembly with two separate hand wheels: one port labeled for vapor and the other labeled for liquid.
  3. Non-refillable (disposable) cylinders are built strictly to ship virgin (new) refrigerant from the factory and must never be used for recovery.
  4. A fusible plug is a cylinder pressure-relief safety fitting filled with a special metal alloy engineered to have a low melting point so it melts and vents pressure during a fire.
  5. Because cylinder pressure and temperature move in the same direction, a decrease (drop) in surrounding temperature causes the pressure inside a cylinder to decrease—not rise.
  6. When moving any cylinder heavier than 35 lb (16 kg) on a wheeled hand truck, safety rules require strapping the tank firmly to the cart using a chain.

Section 2: Pressure Gauges & Vacuum Measurement (11.2)

  1. Inside a mechanical dial gauge, a curved C-shaped metal Bourdon tube flexes and straightens out as internal pressure rises, moving the pointer needle higher on the scale.
  2. The blue low-side compound gauge is unique because it reads two different pressure ranges: positive pressure above atmospheric pressure (psig) and vacuum below atmospheric pressure (in. Hg).
  3. To monitor condensing/discharge pressures on the high side of a system, technicians use the red high-pressure gauge, which reads along a single continuous pressure scale.
  4. To prevent permanent damage or loss of accuracy, a mechanical gauge should not be subjected to pressures exceeding 75% of its full-scale capacity for more than a few minutes.
  5. On a standard low-side vacuum scale at sea level, a complete (perfect) vacuum is represented by a reading of 29.92 in. Hg (inches of mercury).
  6. Because an absolute pressure gauge (such as a digital micron gauge) uses true zero vacuum as its baseline rather than local barometric pressure, it does not need to be adjusted for changes in elevation.

Section 3: Service Valves & System Access (11.3)

  1. Turning a three-way service valve stem all the way counterclockwise places it in the back-seated position, which shuts off the gauge port and is the required position for normal system operation (not front-seated).
  2. A suction service valve bolts directly onto the compressor at its inlet and connects it to the incoming low-pressure suction line.
  3. Mounted on the liquid storage tank outlet, the liquid receiver service valve (king valve) controls refrigerant flow entering the inlet of a liquid line.
  4. When servicing small sealed (hermetic) appliances that do not have factory Schrader ports, the most widely used way to tap into the tubing is installing a piercing valve.
  5. Because a Schrader valve core restricts gas flow inside a service fitting, technicians should remove it from the access port before evacuating a system.

Section 4: Gauge Manifolds & Operation (11.4)

  1. A complete gauge manifold set combines a low-pressure compound gauge and a high-pressure gauge onto a single valved block with connecting hoses.
  2. While using a manifold gauge set to read normal low-side and high-side system pressures, both the low-side and high-side manifold hand valves must remain closed.
  3. When pulling a vacuum to evacuate an entire system through a gauge manifold, both manifold hand valves must be turned open (never closed) so both sides of the system can be evacuated together.

Section 5: Leak Detection Equipment & Methods (11.5)

  1. Modern electronic leak detectors are designed to sense multiple refrigerant types (including HFCs, HCFCs, HFOs, and blends), so it is false that they can only detect CFCs.
  2. In a halide torch leak detector, drawing halogen refrigerant vapors across a glowing copper element causes the flame to change color to light green.
  3. To pinpoint hard-to-find leaks visually, a technician can circulate refrigerant dye that has been injected into the system.
  4. Finding leaks with fluorescent tracer dye requires shining an ultraviolet (UV) (or blue) inspection lamp—not a halogen light—to make the escaping dye glow.
  5. Because an ultrasonic leak detector listens for the high-pitched acoustic hiss of escaping gas, loud shop or mechanical background noise can trigger a false alarm.

Section 6: Vacuum Pumps & System Evacuation (11.6)

  1. Technicians connect a vacuum pump to pull an extremely deep vacuum (evacuating the system) so that trapped moisture (water) boils into vapor and is removed from the system.
  2. The pumping speed or volume rate at which gas can be moved through a vacuum pump is called its free air displacement (typically rated in CFM).
  3. The deepest (highest) vacuum level that a specific vacuum pump is capable of pulling under sealed conditions is rated as its ultimate vacuum.
  4. Inside a rotary-vane vacuum pump, the specialized vacuum pump oil seals internal clearances and serves as a lubricant for moving parts.
  5. If vacuum pump oil absorbs too much water (moisture) during system evacuation, the oil visibly turns white and foamy and must be replaced immediately.

Section 7: Recovery, Recycling, Reclaiming & Charging Scales (11.7)

  1. Refrigerant recovery is the process of extracting refrigerant from a cooling system and transferring it straight into an external storage cylinder (container).
  2. To protect equipment and prevent mixing contaminants between jobs, the filter-drier on a recovery/recycling machine should be changed each time the machine is used.
  3. Unlike recovery or recycling, refrigerant is never (not) reclaimed on the jobsite; reclamation requires factory/laboratory processing and chemical testing to meet AHRI-700 new-refrigerant standards.
  4. Before reprocessed refrigerant can be certified as reclaimed, 100% of all moisture and particulates must be removed from it.
  5. To calculate how much refrigerant is inside a tank using a digital charging scale, a technician must know the cylinder's tare weight, which is how much the cylinder weighs when completely empty.

Key Vocabulary Study Guide: Ch. 11 — Equipment and Instruments for Refrigerant Handling and Service (43 Terms)

Textbook Key Terms (43): Review each term and definition alongside your study guide blanks. Terms marked EPA 608 also reinforce EPA Section 608 certification topics.

Fusible plug§11.1 · p.188EPA 608
A fusible plug is a plug made of a metal with a low melting point, which melts and releases a cylinder's entire refrigerant charge if the cylinder begins to overheat.
Recovery cylinder§11.1 · p.189EPA 608
Recovery cylinders are refrigerant cylinders specifically dedicated to storing refrigerant recovered from refrigeration systems.
Storage cylinder§11.1 · p.188
Storage cylinders are fitted with a valve and a protective cap.
Bourdon tube§11.2 · p.190
A Bourdon tube is a thin-walled tube of elastic metal bent into a circular shape that straightens as pressure inside it increases.
Compound gauge§11.2 · p.192
A compound gauge measures pressure above atmospheric and vacuum.
High-pressure gauge§11.2 · p.193
High-pressure gauges are also known as high-side gauges.
Micron§11.2 · p.192
A micron is the equivalent of 0.001 mm Hg.
Pressure gauge§11.2 · p.190
Pressure gauges are instruments that measure and display the pressure of a fluid in a container.
Retarder§11.2 · p.191
Some pressure gauges use a built-in retarder with the Bourdon tube to measure readings at higher pressures.
Vacuum gauge§11.2 · p.191
A vacuum gauge measures lower-than-atmospheric pressure.
Access port§11.3 · p.200
Access ports are installed in systems where the need for more convenient service outweighs the advantage of having a minimum number of connections.
Back seated§11.3 · p.197EPA 608
When a service valve is back seated, it closes off its service port from the rest of the system, so no pressure readings or procedures can be performed.
Cracked open§11.3 · p.198EPA 608
When a low-side service valve is cracked open only slightly off the back-seated position, a technician can take low-side pressure readings with a compound gauge during normal system operation.
Discharge service valve (DSV)§11.3 · p.198EPA 608
A discharge service valve (DSV) is a high-side service valve that is mounted at a compressor's discharge port, providing a shutoff between the compressor and the condenser.
Front seated§11.3 · p.197EPA 608
When a service valve is front seated, it blocks the flow of refrigerant through the valve by closing off its regular passageway.
King valve§11.3 · p.199EPA 608
On the job, you may hear someone call a liquid receiver service valve a king valve or a receiver outlet valve.
Liquid line service valve§11.3 · p.199
A liquid line service valve is a high-side service valve located in a refrigeration system's condenser and liquid line.
Liquid receiver service valve (LRSV)§11.3 · p.199EPA 608
A liquid receiver service valve (LRSV) is a high-side service valve connected to the outlet of a liquid receiver and the inlet of a liquid line.
Mid-position§11.3 · p.198
Mid-position is used for system evacuation or standing pressure tests.
Piercing valve§11.3 · p.200EPA 608
A piercing valve is a valve that is secured to a length of tubing and accesses the refrigeration system by piercing through the tubing.
Queen valve§11.3 · p.199
King and queen valves are useful when pumping down systems for component isolation.
Schrader valve§11.3 · p.200
A Schrader valve consists of an externally threaded, hollow tube with a spring-loaded, coaxially centered pin that blocks access through the tube.
Service valve§11.3 · p.196
A service valve is a valve with a wrench-operated, movable valve stem that blocks or opens passage through the valve.
Suction line service valve§11.3 · p.197
A suction line service valve is a low-side service valve connected to a refrigeration system's suction line.
Suction service valve (SSV)§11.3 · p.197EPA 608
A suction service valve (SSV) is a low-side service valve that connects to the suction line and directly onto the compressor at its inlet.
Valve core§11.3 · p.202
The valve core should be removed from the valve to prevent damage to the seals during brazing.
Valve core remover§11.3 · p.200
Technicians use a valve core remover that mounts on the access port.
Gauge manifold§11.4 · p.203
A gauge manifold consists of a compound (low-side) gauge, a high-pressure (high-side) gauge, at least three ports that may be isolated from each other, and at least two hand valves.
Refrigeration system analyzer§11.4 · p.207
Refrigeration system analyzers are used to measure system variables, such as pressures and temperatures, and help to determine whether operation is optimal.
Bubble solution§11.5 · p.208
A bubble solution is a soap-water or patented solution that is brushed over an area of tubing that is suspected of leaking.
Electronic leak detection§11.5 · p.209
Electronic leak detection uses electronic sensors to determine if a refrigerant is present.
Fluorescent dye leak detection§11.5 · p.208
Fluorescent dye leak detection uses fluorescent dye and an ultraviolet light to detect leaks.
Halide torch leak detection§11.5 · p.209EPA 608
Halide torch leak detection involves burning a fuel gas near a copper plate and using a rubber hose to siphon air from areas suspected of leaking refrigerant.
Refrigerant dye leak detection§11.5 · p.208EPA 608
Refrigerant dye leak detection involves charging a dye into a refrigeration system and observing areas suspected of leaks during operation.
Ultrasonic leak detector§11.5 · p.210EPA 608
Ultrasonic leak detectors detect the sound that a vapor makes as it is escaping from a pressurized system.
Free air displacement§11.6 · p.211
Free air displacement is the speed at which gas may be pumped through a vacuum pump.
Ultimate vacuum§11.6 · p.211
Ultimate vacuum is the highest vacuum that a vacuum pump can pull.
Vacuum pump§11.6 · p.210
A vacuum pump is a vapor pump used to create vacuums for evacuating a refrigeration system of moisture and other contaminants before refrigerant is recharged into the system.
Digital charging scale§11.7 · p.215EPA 608
A digital charging scale is an electronic refrigerant scale that monitors the weight of a refrigerant cylinder as refrigerant is being charged into or recovered from a system.
Reclaiming§11.7 · p.212EPA 608
The term reclaiming refers to reprocessing a recovered refrigerant so that it is chemically pure.
Recovering§11.7 · p.212EPA 608
The term recovering refers to removing refrigerant from a system and storing it in an external container, regardless of the condition of the removed refrigerant.
Recovery/recycling machine§11.7 · p.214
Many recovery/recycling machines are designed to be versatile and easy to use.
Recycling§11.7 · p.212
Recycling is generally done on a jobsite where the HVACR system is located or at a local service shop.
